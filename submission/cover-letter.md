# Cover letter

Dear Editors,

We submit for consideration in *Forest Policy and Economics* the manuscript **"Deciduous-forward
stocking standards at the allowable cut: value and tail-risk trade-offs in the Prince George Timber
Supply Area."**

The paper asks whether a regulatory allowable annual cut (AAC) is feasible under deciduous-forward
post-clearcut stocking standards, and what those standards cost in value and tail risk at that cut.
Its contribution is methodological and policy-relevant: it links a regulatory ceiling, enterprise
harvest scheduling, species composition, and tail risk in one evaluation. The Prince George Timber
Supply Area is the application, not the contribution.

First, we report the production result. A state-based two-stage convergence is at or above the 2025
AAC of 6.917 million m3/yr in every period under all four standards: 19 periods sit at the cut, and
the final even-flow reference period is 5.3 percent above the ceiling. The 200-year mean is 6.935 to
6.938 million m3/yr, 0.26 to 0.31 percent above the ceiling. The estate can sustain the cut, and the
standards do not reduce it.

Second, we compare the standards in value and tail risk for the three prescriptions D26, D55, and
D84 (26, 55, and 84 percent deciduous targets). At the BC Ministry of Forests 4 percent real rate,
D26 raises net present value by 36.0 million CAD and reduces conditional value at risk at the 20
percent level (CVaR20) loss by 23.4 to 23.7 million CAD, a net gain of 59.4 to 59.7 million CAD.
D55 is approximately break-even: it costs 39.3 million CAD and reduces CVaR20 loss by 38.8 to 47.2
million CAD. D84 costs 146.5 million CAD for a similar reduction and is dominated by D55 on net
CVaR. The observed fire record gives the larger absolute benefit for D55 and D84; for D26 the two
scenarios are nearly identical.

Third, we report the rate dependence. At 3 percent no standard is net-positive; at 4 percent D26 is
the value option and D55 the tail option; at 5 to 8 percent all three are net-positive, and D55 is
best at 8 percent. D84 is behind D55 on net CVaR at every rate in both fire-size scenarios.

Fourth, we state the fire-model boundary. The observed 1,073-fire record and a lower-severity
calibrated-retention sample span the fire-size range we consider. The composition-aware fuel
projection reproduces 63.1 percent of the 2025 VRI fuel-type grid at t=0; the post-harvest slash and
open herb/grass types have no observational counterpart, and the D84 standard's deciduous (D1)
severity multiplier is the model's 0.2 severity floor, not an estimate of D1 severity.

We believe the paper fits *Forest Policy and Economics* because it treats the AAC as a regulatory
instrument, models the enterprise response explicitly, and evaluates composition policy with a
tail-risk criterion rather than a volume target. It also contributes a reproducible method for
testing whether a cut is feasible before comparing policy instruments at that cut.

The manuscript is original and is not under consideration elsewhere. The authors declare no
financial competing interests; one author develops the open-source WS4 estate model used in the
work, which is declared in the manuscript.

Yours sincerely,
